# Chapter3_CommunitiesAfterBarrierRemovalsEU

This is the location where the data and code corresponding to my PhD _**Chapter 3 (Communities Recover After Barrier Removals in European River Basins)**_ are stored.

**Abstract**

River fragmentation is widespread across Europe and accelerating programmes of barrier removal seek to restore longitudinal connectivity within freshwater networks. However, the ecological effects of existing barrier pressure and subsequent barrier removal remain difficult to generalise beyond individual restoration projects and focal species. Here, we integrated long-term riverine invertebrate community data with European barrier inventories, barrier-removal records, river-network topology, climate, and human population pressure to examine community responses to both existing barriers and their removal. Existing-barrier analyses comprised 26,039 site-year observations across 1,772 sites and 397 river sub-basins. Barrier density and barrier type showed no credible association with native taxon richness or the proportion of non-native taxa; instead, community structure was more consistently associated with climatic, anthropogenic, and river-network context, including lower richness with increasing human population density and strong spatial structuring of non-native representation within river networks. In contrast, post-removal analyses of 3,547 observations from 655 sites revealed pronounced temporal recovery. Native taxon richness increased non-linearly with time since removal, with stronger proportional gains among communities with lower pre-removal richness. The proportion of non-native taxa declined through time, with this decline shaped further by network distance, connectivity, and the number of intervening barriers. These findings indicate that static measures of barrier burden alone poorly predict contemporary invertebrate community structure, whereas the removal of individual barriers is followed by detectable, ecologically meaningful recovery—native communities becoming richer and proportionally less dominated by non-native taxa as time since reconnection increases. This consistent, favourable biotic signal suggests that barrier removal is not merely a hydromorphological intervention but an effective and measurable lever for freshwater biodiversity recovery, provided restoration planning accounts for pre-removal ecological baselines and river-network context when prioritising and evaluating future reconnection efforts.

**Keywords**

Biological invasions; connectivity restoration; freshwater invertebrates; river barriers; river restoration; temporal dynamics
